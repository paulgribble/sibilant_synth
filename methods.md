# Methods paragraph: synthesis of the /ʃ/–/s/ continuum

Stimuli were synthesized from each talker's own recordings using a data-driven, talker-specific model (MATLAB R2026a). For each of the 72 talkers, all unshifted (practice and baseline) productions of *she*, *see*, *shoe* and *sue* (44.1 kHz; up to 30 tokens of each /ʃ/ word and 5 of each /s/ word) were segmented at the hand-scored sibilant onset, vowel onset and vowel offset; tokens with clipping, sibilants shorter than 60 ms or vowels shorter than 80 ms were discarded, and for words with at least 10 tokens, duration and level outliers beyond 3 robust standard deviations were removed. The sibilant of each token was characterized by multitaper power spectra (40 ms windows, 7 Slepian tapers) at five equally spaced points across the frication, resampled onto a 1/48-octave grid, averaged across tokens in the power domain, converted to dB and smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (σ = 1/24 octave), together with its RMS envelope (25 points in normalized time), median duration and mean level relative to the following vowel. A spectral landmark was defined per time slice as the power-weighted log-frequency centroid of the region within 6 dB of the in-band maximum (1.8–5 kHz for /ʃ/, 3–10 kHz for /s/). The vowel of each token was modeled by 40th-order linear prediction (pre-emphasis 0.97, 30 ms Hamming windows) in 40 normalized-time frames, converted to line spectral frequencies and averaged across tokens in that domain, which preserves formant bandwidths; the mean RMS contour and the LPC residuals of the two /ʃ/-word tokens closest to the median vowel duration were stored as excitation templates. A token at continuum position *a* (0 = /ʃ/, 1 = /s/) was generated as follows. The sibilant was Gaussian noise shaped by short-time Fourier filtering (1024-point frames, 75% overlap) with a time-varying envelope obtained by morphing, rather than mixing, the talker's mean /ʃ/ and /s/ spectra in the appropriate vowel context: the frequency axis of each spectrum was warped piecewise-linearly on the mel scale (O'Shaughnessy, 1987) so that the two landmarks coincided at mel⁻¹[(1 − *a*)·mel(*f*ʃ) + *a*·mel(*f*s)], after which the levels were interpolated linearly in dB. Equal steps in *a* therefore correspond to equal mel steps of the dominant spectral peak, and intermediate spectra remain unimodal instead of showing the two peaks that a level cross-fade produces. Sibilant duration (log-linear), RMS envelope and level relative to the vowel (linear in dB) were interpolated with *a* as well. The vowel was produced by passing the talker's own LPC residual through the time-varying all-pole filter (updated every 5 ms) and de-emphasizing, so that fundamental frequency, voice quality and duration were those of the talker; the filter and RMS contour were the interpolation, at a fixed weight of 0.5, of the talker's mean vowel after /ʃ/ and after /s/, so that every token of a continuum carried an identical, category-neutral vowel and the tokens differed only in the sibilant (cf. Lane et al., 2007, who concatenated a single natural vowel to every step). Sibilant and vowel were joined with a 10 ms raised-cosine cross-fade, the vowel was scaled to −20 dB FS RMS with the sibilant at its interpolated level relative to it, and 50 ms of silence was added at each end. Acoustic validation against the talkers' real productions showed that the synthetic endpoints lay within each talker's own distribution of mid-sibilant spectra (median RMS z of 0.53 for /ʃ/ and 0.63 for /s/, versus 0.69 and 0.94 for real tokens scored leave-one-out against their peers), that the position of the tokens on each talker's own /ʃ/–/s/ spectral axis increased monotonically with *a* in every talker × vowel cell, and that mid-vowel F1 and F2 of the endpoints matched the talkers' real means to within a median of 7 Hz.

## Relation to the continua of Lane et al. (2007) and Shiller et al. (2009)

The present method departs in several respects from the /s/–/ʃ/ continuum introduced by Lane et al. (2007) and reused by Shiller et al. (2009). Lane et al. synthesized the frication with a Klatt formant synthesizer in parallel mode, fitting the resonance frequencies and amplitudes of F2–F6 by eye to the power spectra of one male and one female talker's natural "said" and "shed", then equating the resonance frequencies of the two endpoints and generating the continuum by interpolating only the resonance amplitudes (A2F–A6F), on the argument that the /s/–/ʃ/ contrast is carried by which vocal-tract resonances are excited by the frication source rather than by their frequencies (Keyser and Stevens, 2006; Stevens, 1998). The synthesizer parameters were constant over the duration of the frication, the top resonance was placed at 6.8–7.2 kHz, and the amplitude steps were re-spaced by hand until normal-hearing pilot listeners labeled half of the stimuli as /s/; a single natural /ɛd/ was concatenated to every step. Our continuum differs first in its source: rather than a parametric approximation to two model talkers, each stimulus set is generated from the measured spectra, vocal-tract filters and glottal residuals of the listener's own recordings, for 72 talkers, with time-varying spectra sampled at five points across the frication and a bandwidth extending to the Nyquist frequency of 22 kHz, which retains the /s/ energy above 8 kHz that lies outside the range of the Klatt implementation. Second, and more fundamentally, the two continua take different paths between the same endpoints. Amplitude interpolation between resonances at fixed frequencies passes through broad, nearly flat spectra (at the midpoint of Lane et al.'s female continuum the F3 and F5 amplitudes differ by 5 dB), so that an intermediate stimulus is ambiguous because it is uninformative; the intermediate resonance that an intermediate constriction would excite (F4 in their parameterization) is barely changed along their continuum (A4F 45 to 50 dB). In the present talkers the premise of fixed, shared resonances holds only asymmetrically: a secondary peak near the talker's /s/ landmark was present in 62% of the /ʃ/ spectra, but a secondary peak near the /ʃ/ landmark was present in only 27% of the /s/ spectra, where that region is simply the low-frequency skirt of the spectrum, on average 19 dB below the peak. Applied to measured rather than parametric spectra, amplitude interpolation therefore reduces to a level cross-fade, which yields bimodal intermediate spectra. The landmark morph instead moves the dominant spectral peak continuously between the talker's /ʃ/ and /s/ positions (about 3.3 and 6.7 kHz on median, one octave apart) in equal mel steps, so that an intermediate stimulus resembles a production with an intermediate place of constriction and the continuum lies along the same frequency axis as the spectral shift imposed on auditory feedback in the production task (Shiller et al., 2009), allowing a perceptual boundary shift to be expressed in the units of the perturbation. Third, whereas Lane et al. spaced their steps psychometrically and extended the continuum beyond the endpoints with deliberately poor exemplars, our steps are spaced acoustically (equal mel intervals of the landmark, with duration, level and envelope interpolated alongside) and the endpoints are the talker's own mean productions, which the acoustic validation places inside that talker's distribution of natural tokens. We retain from Lane et al. and Shiller et al. the decision to hold the vowel constant across the continuum, implemented here not by concatenating one natural token but by fixing the talker's vocal-tract filter halfway between its /ʃ/- and /s/-context estimates so that the vowel favors neither category, and, as they did, we leave the placement of the category boundary within the continuum to be established by listening.
